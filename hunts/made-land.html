Pack 05

Back Bay Landfill and Fens Walking Tour

Most of the Back Bay was water until the 1850s. Not marsh at the edges - open tidal flats, walled off by a dam in 1821, poisoned by the city's own sewers, and then filled with dirt and gravel hauled in by train from Needham. Made Land is a self-guided riddle hunt across that ground, from the Public Garden to the Back Bay Fens, about two and a half hours on foot.

Each riddle walks you to a real thing you can stand next to, and the answer is something only visible on site. Then the story: what happened here, who said so, and where the famous version gets it wrong. Some of the stops are landmarks you know, told from underneath. Others are things almost nobody notices - like the small round metal caps in Back Bay sidewalks, which belong to a citywide network of groundwater wells watching over the wooden pilings that hold up the neighborhood's churches and rowhouses.

You'll see where subway workers found fish traps that predate the pyramids, where Olmsted built a flood basin and refused to call it a park, why a dam in 1910 killed the salt marsh he planted, and which famous Boston building had its foundation rot out from under it. Every fact is sourced on our sources page: Landmarks Commission reports, the Groundwater Trust's own records, a 1903 state committee on the Charles River, the Board of Health's 1878 map of the city's smells.

The route is flat, step-free, and one-way, starting at Arlington station and ending near the Museum of Fine Arts on the Green Line, with a bonus stop upstream on the Muddy River. There's also a one-hour short cut from Arlington station through Copley to Commonwealth Avenue at Fairfield Street. Walk it, look down, and see the bay that used to be here.

Play any two stops free.

Stop 01 · Public Garden

The Public Garden sits on city trash, dumped over a rope yard, on a mud flat.

You are standing on old city trash. The garden's first gardener, John Cadness, is quoted in the Landmarks Commission's report: the land sat four to six feet below the street, filled in with all sorts of city refuse, and still open to the tide. Before that it was a rope factory on a mud flat. In 1794 the town gave the marsh west of the Common to the ropemakers, whose ropewalks downtown had burned. Six long sheds went up, burned in turn, and were rebuilt in 1806. In 1824 the city bought the land back for $55,000. Horace Gray and friends petitioned in 1837 for a botanic garden here. You will read that it was the first public botanical garden in America; the Cultural Landscape Foundation says so and offers no proof. The bridge has its own claims. Its plaque says it opened in 1867; the same Landmarks report says it went in in 1869. It gets billed as the world's shortest suspension bridge, but Bridgemeister lists shorter ones in Germany and says a steel girder has been the main support since 1921. The cables are mostly costume. The engineer on the plaque, Clemens Herschel, went on to patent the Venturi meter in 1888, for measuring water in a pipe. Everything on this walk is about water you can't see.

Look now

Read the bronze tablet on the bridge, then look at the cables. Since 1921 a steel girder has done most of the work.

Stop 02 · Back Bay

Arlington Street Church stands on 999 logs, and a lid in the sidewalk checks they're still wet.

Nine hundred ninety-nine wooden logs hold this church up, by its own count, and the only thing keeping them from rotting is water that a lid in the sidewalk measures. The church says it was the first public building in the Back Bay, begun in September 1859 and dedicated on December 11, 1861, while the fill around it was still fresh. Arthur Gilman modeled the outside on St Martin-in-the-Fields in London. Wood piles last as long as they stay underwater; exposed to air, they rot. By 1890, according to a 1990 city-commissioned well study, the rules said untreated piles had to be cut off below elevation plus five on Boston City Base, so they would stay wet. This church is older than that rule. The round cap beside it is observation well 23J-1934B in the Boston Groundwater Trust's public database. On September 23, 2026, the water in it stood at 5.87. The lowest reading since records began here in 2007 was 3.09, that October; the highest, 8.57 in September 2021. If this church's piles were cut at plus five, that 2007 low left about two feet of them above the water. Nobody has published where they actually stop, and the Trust says cut-offs vary from building to building. The readings are online. Almost nobody looks.

Look now

Find the round cap in the Arlington Street sidewalk; you are standing over the water that holds this church up.
