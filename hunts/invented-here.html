Pack 08

A Walking Tour of Boston Firsts - and Which Ones Are True

Boston likes to be first. The telephone, the public school, the public library, Boston cream pie, the bargain basement, the disposable razor blade - the city has a plaque, a sign or a hotel brochure for each of them. Invented Here walks you to thirteen of those claims and then reads the paperwork.

It starts in the Blackstone Block at an oyster bar that calls itself the oldest restaurant in America, and ends on a bridge over the Fort Point Channel, looking at a razor plant that has been making blades on the water for more than a century. In between: the garret where Bell first heard sound come down a wire, the hotel that kept its bathtubs in the basement, the corner where a chef who may never have existed supposedly invented a dessert, the school Boston's richer inhabitants chipped in for, Filene's price clock, the theater Edison wired, the library that opened its reading room before it lent a book, the boarding-house sentence two men remembered two different ways, and the corner with a marker for a political monster.

Each stop is a riddle that walks you to the next corner, and the answer is something you can only get by looking: a year on an awning, a word on a plaque, a name on a marker. Then you get the story - the legend first, then what the town records, patents, landmark files and the institutions' own historians actually say. Some claims hold up. Most need an asterisk. A few are flatly wrong, and we tell you who said so.

It's about two and three-quarter hours and five kilometers, mostly flat and step-free, from Haymarket to Broadway station. There's a one-hour short cut from Government Center to the Theatre District, a bonus stop on the Common, and two T-ride side trips: the chocolate mills at Lower Mills and the Cambridgeport bakery where the Fig Newton was born.

Any two stops are free. Every fact links to its source.

Stop 01 · Blackstone Block / Downtown

America's 'oldest restaurant' turned two hundred in 2026. Its own landmark file can't say when the building went up.

The Union Oyster House calls itself America's oldest restaurant, and it has the stories to go with it. A future king of France lived upstairs in 1796 and taught French to pay the rent. Isaiah Thomas printed the Massachusetts Spy here. A man named Charles Forster brought the toothpick to America by paying Harvard students to ask for one. Now read the nomination that made it a National Historic Landmark in 2003. It says nobody knows when the building went up: deed research points to about 1716 or 1717, and other writers say 1704 or 1730. The Louis Philippe story is never clearly sourced. The Spy was printed here 'possibly on the third floor,' on the word of a 1961 commission report and a 1957 guidebook. The toothpick tale opens with 'tradition holds' and rests on an undated clipping. The 'oldest continually operating restaurant' line has no footnote at all. Even 1826 carries a hedge: whether the first Union Street oyster house was this one is 'likely,' the nomination says, not certain. What it does document is Hawes Atwood opening an oyster house on October 7, 1826. By the awning's count, it turned two hundred on October 7, 2026.

Look now

Look up at the rooftop letters - they went up in 1957, and the landmark nomination notes you can read them from I-93.

Stop 02 · Government Center

On June 2, 1875, a stuck spring twanged down a wire in a Court Street garret. Thomas Edison had tinkered in the same shop seven years earlier.

The famous sentence, 'Mr. Watson, come here,' didn't happen here. That was nine months later, across downtown, and you'll get there. What happened here was smaller and earlier. Charles Williams ran an electrical shop at 109 Court Street - in Watson's words, 'one of the largest and best fitted shops in the country.' Up in its 'hot garret,' Watson was tuning reed transmitters for Bell's harmonic telegraph on June 2, 1875, when one spring stuck. He plucked it. In the next room Bell heard the twang come through his receiver, and came rushing out: 'What did you do then? Don't change anything. Let me see!' That was sound, not speech, but it told Bell the idea worked. The plaque calls it the first time sound went over wires. That's first for Bell: in October 1861 a Frankfurt physics teacher, Philipp Reis, had shown an electric telephone to the city's Physical Society. Seven years before the twang, a twenty-one-year-old telegrapher named Thomas Edison had done his own experimenting in this same shop. His vote recorder, patented June 1, 1869, was the first invention he ever patented. Congress didn't want it. The building is long gone.

Look now

The plaque puts the garret on the fifth floor. Count five floors up the federal building and picture a hot attic full of wire.

Detours

  • First Chocolate Factory, Not First Chocolate
    From South Station, Red Line outbound on the Ashmont branch to Ashmont (about 20 minutes), then the Mattapan trolley three stops to Milton. The mill buildings are just across the Neponset on the Dorchester side. Come back the same way. Subway fare applies - check trolley service before you go.
  • Named for the Town, Not the Scientist
    From Broadway, Red Line inbound (toward Alewife) to Central, about 15 minutes. Walk south on Prospect Street, right on Franklin Street, about 10 minutes to number 129. Subway fare applies.
