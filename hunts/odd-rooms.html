Pack 03

Boston's Oddest Rooms: A Free and Nearly Free Walk

This is a walk past Boston's oddest rooms. It's worth it from the sidewalk, better if you go in, and several of them are free. It runs from the north slope of Beacon Hill through the Common and Back Bay to the Fens, about two and three quarter hours, and every stop has an answer you can find on the street, at noon or at 9 p.m.

You'll stand outside a synagogue whose pews came from a Black church, and a Black meeting house that became a synagogue. You'll hear how Harvard students walked the State House's wooden codfish out in a flower box. You'll find a library with half a street number, a little stone building on the Common that a parks commissioner modeled on Versailles, and then the house he froze in 1936. Under a church on Newbury Street is a basement that lends out twelve-foot dragons. Then the genealogists the state evicted, a painting the legislature voted to seize, what its preservationists call the only Louis Comfort Tiffany house still standing, and a glass globe stuck in 1935.

Where it matters, each stop says whether going in is free or ticketed. Hours change, so check before you go. Three optional detours are a T ride away: the Kennedy Library on a former dump, the house where Rose Kennedy re-created her son's birthplace from memory, and a museum of bad art in a brewery that's open late.

Every fact is sourced and listed on our sources page. When a famous story is shaky, we tell the story and then what the records say.

Any two stops are free. Walk them and decide.

Stop 01 · Beacon Hill (north slope)

The pews in this synagogue came from a Black Baptist church down the street.

The wooden pews in this synagogue came from a Black church. Jewish immigrants from the province around Vilna, now Vilnius in Lithuania, organized on Beacon Hill in 1888, and their first real home was the old Twelfth Baptist Church at 45 Phillips Street, a Black congregation's meetinghouse. When the city took that building by eminent domain around 1916 to expand a school and paid them $20,000, they built this one, cornerstone 1919, designed by Max Kalman, whom the West End Museum calls the only Jewish architect in Boston. And they carried the pews over. The West End Museum says those pews had seated people who escaped slavery and men who volunteered for the 54th Massachusetts.

Inside, the megillah holders next to the ark are pickle barrels wrapped in red velvet, per the Forward. The walls carry three layers of Eastern European folk murals, each one painted over before the next. Restorers didn't know they were there until 1998. The last member, Mendel Miller, held the final service in 1985.

Sources disagree on when they bought the Baptist church: 1902 or 1906. Inside opens for tours and events, per the Vilna Shul; check before you go.

Look at the round window, then the door. The pews went in through there.

Stop 02 · Beacon Hill (north slope)

From 1904 to 1972, this Black meeting house was a synagogue.

From 1904 to 1972, this Black meeting house was a synagogue. The Baptist congregation that built it sold it in 1898 and moved to the South End, and by 1904 it was Congregation Anshi Libavitz. It stayed a synagogue until 1972, when the Museum of African American History bought it. So on this short stretch of the north slope, a Black church became a synagogue, and a synagogue was furnished from a Black church. Same neighborhood, two directions.

The tablet by the door names the man who got it built. The National Park Service says Cato Gardner, a native of Africa, personally raised $1,500 toward it, and African American craftsmen did most of the work. It was finished in 1806 for a Baptist congregation that had formed the year before with 24 members, 15 of them women.

It was a school, a lecture hall and a political room as much as a church. William Lloyd Garrison founded the New England Anti-Slavery Society here in 1832. In 1863 it was a recruiting post for the 54th Massachusetts. Going inside takes a reserved ticket from the museum; check before you go.

Detours

  • A Glass Room on a Garbage Dump
    From the State House, walk down to Park Street. Red Line (Ashmont or Braintree) to JFK/UMass, about 15 minutes. Follow signs to the ground-level bus shelters and take the free UMass Boston shuttle (Route 1) to the library. Allow 2 to 3 hours for the round trip and visit.
  • The House a Mother Rebuilt From Memory
    From the MHS, walk to Hynes Convention Center and take the Green Line C branch outbound (toward Cleveland Circle) to Coolidge Corner, about 15 minutes. Walk north on Harvard Street to Beals Street, about 10 minutes. House tours run in season only; check the posted hours before you go. The outside works any time.
  • The Museum of Bad Art, in a Brewery
    From the Common, walk to Park Street and take the Red Line south to Andrew, about 10 minutes. 1250 Massachusetts Avenue is about a 15-minute walk southwest of Andrew Square: look for the tall grain silo with 'Dorchester' in script on top. Open into the evening every day; check the posted hours before you go. 21 and over after 6 p.m. on Fridays and Saturdays.
